# A section is aligned with its successor by title and prose, not by order

*Decided October 2026 (issue #388, split out of #179).* Which section of a successor replaces each section of the document it obsoletes is computed offline, for every obsoletes edge, by `SectionAlignment.pairs(old:new:)` in RFCKit, and shipped as the `successions` table of `indexes.sqlite`. #179's successor marker, #187's coupled scrolling and the diff engine's paragraph alignment all read it.

Every section of either document, at every depth, is scored against every section of the other: Jaccard similarity of the titles' words at 0.4, and the cosine of the two sections' own prose, weighted by how rare each word is across both documents, at 0.6. A word joined by hyphens stays one word, so "Content-Type" does not match "Content". A pair is kept when it scores at least 0.3 and is the best match of one of its two sections. Matching is not one to one, so a split or merged section gets one row for each counterpart. Position breaks only a tie. An alignment that keeps the order, LCS over the sections, was rejected without being built: RFC 9110 moves whole chapters of RFC 7231 (content negotiation goes from §5.3 to §12), and a restructured successor is the case the feature is for.

**How it was measured.** There is no ground truth to measure against. RFC 9110's Appendix B lists, for each document it obsoletes, the 9110 sections that changed, but it names only 9110's side. So the predecessor of each section named there was looked up by hand, and the labels are written as section numbers only (`Corpus-backed: section alignment`). That gives 35 labeled pairs over RFC 7230, 7231, 7232, 7233 and 7538. It also gives six sections labeled as having no predecessor, such as 421 and 422, which came from other documents, and leaves out four whose counterpart is unclear. The threshold and the weights were chosen for the most recall that keeps precision at 90% or more: 31 of 33 claimed pairs are right, and 31 of the 35 are found. Weighting the two parts equally, at 0.35, claims 29, all right, but finds only 29. Precision was put first because a wrong "replaced by" misleads, while a missing one falls back to the document-level status. The test holds precision at 90% and recall at 85%, so a change that loses either fails.

Over the whole corpus (9,835 documents), the second pass of `corpus-build index` writes 50,027 rows for 1,440 obsoletes edges. The whole run, both passes, takes 93 seconds in a release build, and the table adds 4.9 MB to the database, and 1.1 MB to its LZFSE archive (3.5 to 4.6 MB). Precision outside RFC 9110 is not measured. Most edges are a new revision of the same document, where alignment is easy, but the label set is one document, and the first consumer that shows a marker should check a sample of other pairs.
