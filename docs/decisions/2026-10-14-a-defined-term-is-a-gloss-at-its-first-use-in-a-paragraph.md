# A defined term is a gloss at its first use in a paragraph, not a link

*Decided 14 October 2026 (issue #396), with the agreements made on #176 on 28 September.*

The reader marks a term the document defines (`RFCDocument.definedTerms`, #176) where the document uses it, so its definition can be read without leaving the place: a dotted underline in the secondary color, quieter than a link's, the long-standing convention for a defined term or an abbreviation.

**It is a gloss, not a link.** A marked term carries `.rfcGloss`, a `GlossBox` per use, and no `.link`: a click on it selects, as on any text. Its card shows the term and its definition on hover and force click on macOS and on long press on iOS, as a reference's card does (the 26 September decision). **Go to Definition** in its menu, the Mac's context menu and the long-press menu, follows the definition's anchor, so Back returns. Hover waits 2 seconds for a gloss's card rather than a reference's half second: a gloss is a word of the prose the pointer passes over as it reads, and a force click shows it at once (the maintainer's choice). A link to the definition was the cheaper choice, all of a reference's handling reused; the maintainer chose the gloss, which abbreviations are to share (#67), and which has no anchor of its own to link to there. A hover target is therefore a reference or a gloss (`HoverTarget`), told apart by its box.

**Only the first use in each paragraph is marked**, matched case-sensitively, as a whole word, the longest term where two start at the same word. Not in a heading, a table cell, a definition list's term, inline code, a link or a reference, nor in the term's own definition; and not on paper, where there is no card to show.

**A term of one word that is a common English word is not marked.** `client` or `message` is known to every reader, defined by nearly every document its own way, and would be underlined in nearly every paragraph. The common words are the lower-case words used in prose in at least a tenth of the corpus's documents, `corpus-build common-words` over the 9,835 documents of 14 October: 1,606 words, committed as RFCReaderKit's `CommonWords.txt`, a word list and no RFC text. An acronym is never one, being no lower-case word, and a term of several words is always marked, whatever its words.

| Share of documents | Common words | Defined single-word spellings skipped (of 2,190) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5% | 8,395 | 111 |
| 2% | 4,530 | 91 |
| 5% | 2,650 | 72 |
| **10%** | **1,606** | **51** |
| 20% | 797 | 26 |
| 30% | 452 | 17 |

At a tenth it skips the vocabulary of every protocol, `key`, `address`, `message`, `server`, `client`, `header`, `packet`, `connection`, `peer`, `byte`, `proxy`, `gateway`, `endpoint`, and keeps what a document defines that a reader benefits from: `upstream` and `downstream` (8.5%), `datagram`, `handshake`, `relay`, `cipher`, `plaintext`. The threshold is `COMMON_WORDS_THRESHOLD` in the Makefile; `corpus/common-words.json` reports the others.

The bar to show terms at all was 95% of a hand-read sample defined correctly; #840 measured 98 of 100.

## How the terms are collected

*Moved here from ARCHITECTURE.md on 17 October 2026 (#756).*

Defined terms are collected at parse time (`DefinedTerms`, issue #176) into `RFCDocument.definedTerms`, keyed by each spelling of the term prose would use (`DefinedTerms.spellings(of:)`, #396): quotes, a citation, the start of a definition after `: ` and a trailing dash the term carried are left off, `X (Y)` is two spellings where one side is a short form the other expands whole (`Abbreviations.longForm`, as a glossary entry is read) and a qualifier left off where not, and a definition list's term that is a comma list, split only at a comma outside brackets, is the spellings of each item, cleaned the same way. The first definition wins, except that one with a definition replaces one without, and a term left without a definition is dropped, having nothing to show. Two sources, strictest first: a primary index entry (`<iref primary="true">`) without a subitem; the XML says only which anchors it may be defined at, innermost first, and the built model says which it holds (`DefinedTerms.lookUp`, #455), its block being the definition, or, where that anchor defines nothing itself (a section, a table or a table row), the block the entry sits in (one in a `<dt>` is defined by the `<dd>` after it) (a subitem files a name under an index group, `Grammar` / `ALPHA`, which is not a term); and the definition lists of a section titled for its terms and of its subsections, since most definition lists describe fields or notation. A title names terms when it holds Terminology, Glossary or the noun Terms (`New Terms`, `Terms Used in This Document`), is `Conventions and` followed by Definitions, Terminology, Terms or Acronyms, opens with Definitions, is `Definition of Terms`, or lists Definitions as an item of its own (`Symbols, Abbreviations, and Definitions`). `Conventions and Notation` describes notation, as a bare `Conventions` does. Definitions qualified by a word before it is a format's, an option's or a media type's as often as a document's terms, and `General` or `Technical` cannot be told apart from `Field` or `Option`, so all of them are left out. Measured over the corpus, that and dropping the singular took 484 terms out of 41 documents, nearly all of them fields, parameters and registration-template entries; the genuine terminology left out with them sits under a qualified title. A list nested in a definition describes that term's parts and is not read (21 terms in 7 documents); one set in a list item is. An index entry that has no definition text, one placed directly in a section, gives way to an entry that has, so a term an index marks and a terminology list defines lands on the list item. A subsection inherits its parent's title, because a terminology section splits its lists into `Core Terms`, `Abbreviations` or `Roles` as readily as it titles them for terms; that brought in 903 terms in 55 documents, most of them abbreviation lists, at the cost of 25 that are not terms: RFC 8891 and 9374's notation symbols, and the graph notation and per-term Definition/Discussion headings of RFC 9912, 9914 and 9971. A title that only opens with Definitions passes nothing on, since it can head a specification's body: it brought in RFC 8985's connection variables and timers and RFC 9208's command arguments (23 terms), and nothing else. The noun Terms brought in 424 terms in 30 documents, all but nine of them terminology (RFC 8985's two variables and RFC 9224's imported JSON grammar names). Narrowing `Conventions and` took nothing out: the four documents titled `Conventions and Notation`, `…Assumptions` or `…Licenses` hold no definition list there. Free prose is not read. Over the corpus, 466 documents defined 5,991 terms as written, and 464 define 6,170 spellings once cleaned. A hand-read sample of 100 was 80 right before the spellings were cleaned, every error in the term's form rather than its definition, and 98 right after (#396, which has the counts).
