# Architecture

## Shape of the code base

```
rfc-reader/
├── Packages/RFCKit/          Swift package: everything that is not UI. Builds and tests on Linux and macOS.
│   ├── Sources/RFCKit/
│   │   ├── Models/           DocumentID, RFCMetadata, RFCIndex, enums for status/stream/format
│   │   ├── Index/            RFCIndexParser (streaming SAX), XMLTree (small DOM used by the document parser)
│   │   ├── Document/         RFCDocument model, RFCXMLParser (RFCXML v3), LegacyTextParser (plain text), RFCXMLSerializer
│   │   ├── Client/           RFCEditorEndpoints, RFCEditorClient, RFCLink (URL scheme + web URLs), feed parser
│   │   ├── Citation/         CitationFormatter (short, full, Markdown, BibTeX, URL)
│   │   └── Search/           IndexSearch (in-memory metadata search with a small query grammar), SearchQuery (that grammar written back out, and completed)
│   └── Tests/RFCKitTests/    Swift Testing suites with real fixtures (RFC 1149, 2119, 5234, 8999, index sample, RSS, JSON)
├── Packages/RFCReaderKit/    Swift package: everything in the app that is a pure function of its inputs — DocumentTextBuilder, FragmentGeometry, ReaderLayout, link routing, DocumentStore (actor, disk cache), the stores and the SwiftData schema. Needs an Apple SDK.
├── Tools/corpus-build/       Offline pipeline (fetch, convert to RFCXML, manifest; the checks in RFCCorpusKit); see DATA_PIPELINE.md
├── App/RFCReader/            SwiftUI multiplatform app (iOS, iPadOS, macOS); no test bundle, so nothing testable lives here
│   ├── Model/                LibraryModel (process-wide), NavigationModel (per tab), ReaderState (per window)
│   ├── Views/                Navigation, list, reader, inspector
│   │   └── Rendering/        RFCTextView, RFCTextViewCoordinator, RFCTextLayoutFragment — one TextKit 2 text view over one document text storage
│   ├── Window/               macOS only: AppDelegate, ReaderWindowController, the toolbar
│   ├── Scripting/            macOS only: the AppleScript dictionary
│   └── Intents/              App Intents (Open RFC)
├── project.yml               XcodeGen spec that produces RFCReader.xcodeproj
└── docs/
```

The split is the point: **RFCKit knows nothing about SwiftUI, and the app knows nothing about XML or the 72-column text format.** The boundary is the `RFCDocument` model. That keeps the hard, testable logic on a fast Linux/macOS test loop and lets the UI be rewritten (TextKit 2, a different navigation model, visionOS) without touching parsing.

## The document model

```
RFCDocument
├── header: DocumentHeader          title, id, authors, date, abstract blocks, keywords, obsoletes/updates, precedingDraft
├── sections: [Section]             tree; each has anchor, number ("4.2", "A.1"), title: [Inline], blocks, subsections
└── source: .xml | .text

Block (enum)
  paragraph(Paragraph)              inlines + optional anchor + author's indent in characters
  list(ListBlock)                   bullet / numbered(ListNumbering) / bare; items hold blocks
  definitionList(DefinitionList)    items (term inlines + definition blocks); compact, and whether terms hang beside their definitions
  preformatted(Preformatted)        artwork or sourceCode, verbatim text, optional language
  figure(Figure) · table(Table) · blockQuote · aside
  references(ReferenceList)         bibliographic entries with resolved DocumentID where possible, and annotation inlines; its kind (normative, informative, unknown) is derived from its title

Inline (enum)
  text · emphasis · strong · code · superscript · subscript · link(URL) · crossReference · lineBreak

CrossReference.target
  .anchor(String)                                          same document
  .document(DocumentID, section: String?, entry: String?)  another RFC, optionally a section, and the bibliography entry the citation resolved to
  .entrySection(entry:, tag:, section:, url: URL?)          a section of a bibliography entry outside the series, and that section's page
```

Design choices worth knowing:

- **Anchors are stable strings**, not indices. XML documents use the author's `anchor` or the RFC Editor's `pn` part number; text documents synthesize `section-4.2`, `appendix-A`, `name-security-considerations`. A bibliography entry keeps its label when the label is an XML name (`RFC2119`, `MIP-OPTIM`); one that is not (`[1]`, `[RFC 2119]`) is anchored under the document it cites (`RFC791`) or else `ref-` and the label spelled as a name, and keeps the label as `displayAnchor` either way. Entry anchors are settled before the prose is linked: a repeat takes the first `-2`, `-3` no other entry or section holds, and a label listed twice is cited as its first entry. Deep links, the table of contents and reading positions all key off these. Whatever a document can cite keeps its anchor, down to a `<dd>` and a table row, header or body (#166); `Block.anchors` lists the ones a block carries, and `[Block].flattened` walks the nested blocks, so the check that every anchor a document cites is one the model holds walks the same blocks `referencedDocuments` does.
- **Cross references are resolved at parse time.** The XML parser indexes `<reference>` anchors first, in every `<references>` wherever it sits, not only `<back>`'s, so `<xref target="QUIC-TRANSPORT">` becomes `.document(RFC9000)`. XML from elsewhere can hold a list in a chapter or in `<middle>`, and so could a converted legacy document until #315: the serializer now lifts every bibliography outside the back into it, keeping its anchor, number and name, in document order before the back's sections, and wraps a list's own entries in a nested list named after it where it also holds lists of its own, which is the one shape the schema accepts there. A numbered section that follows the last bibliography goes into the back with it and keeps a section's `pn`, and the parser reads a `<back>` section by its `pn`, by its place only where it has none, so that section is not announced as an appendix (#683). What the serializer writes for a legacy document is a fixed point: parsed and written again with the same options, it is the same bytes, so a patched document changes only where its patch does (`SerializerRoundTripTests`; over the corpus, all but the 7 documents of #686). That holds because the legacy parser builds what reading the XML gives, not because the serializer copies the XML parser's rules: titles with their whitespace collapsed, a URL into the RFC series as a citation (in `InlineLinker`, which both parsers run), a definition's term linked like its text. Where RFCXML can only say something one way, the serializer says it so: a label composed from the document, written against an entry whose tag is another, names the document in the xref's words, which the parser reads as the document's label. The text parser does the same with `[RFC2119]`-style entries in the References section, then linkifies `[Anchor]`, `RFC 1234`, `Section 4.2 of [RFC9110]`, `Section 3` (only if that section exists) and URLs in prose.
- **Abbreviations are collected at parse time too** (`Abbreviations`, issue #67), from the document's own text: `Transport Layer Security (TLS)` at first use, and `TLS: Transport Layer Security` in a glossary definition list, where the whole opening phrase has to be the expansion. Both parsers set `RFCDocument.abbreviations` as their last step, keyed by the short form as written, and the first expansion wins. A section's heading is read before its body, as the reader meets it, so `3. Transport Layer Security (TLS)` gives the body's `TLS` its expansion, defined in that section; a heading's long form set all in capitals, as legacy headings often are (`RECIPIENT (RCPT)`), is kept only until a later expansion in mixed case, from the body or a later heading, replaces it. The long form is found with Schwartz and Hearst's algorithm, chosen for precision: every letter of the short form has to match, in order, and its first has to start a word, or there is no expansion. A wrong expansion shown confidently is worse than none. Four rules from a full-corpus measurement tighten it. An expansion never starts on a lowercase function word (`and Control of TE Networks (ACTN)` moves back to `Abstraction`); the word it moves back to cannot be a function word either, capitalized or not, and every other word it takes in has to begin with a letter of the short form, in order, so `all routers in a Border Network (ABN)` has no expansion rather than a wrong one. A hyphenated word is one word throughout, so neither a match nor that move lands inside `peer-to-peer`. Nothing holding `=`, `@` or `<` is an expansion. And a glossary phrase ends at `:`, at a dash set off by spaces (` -- `, ` - `) or an em dash, and before its trailing citations (`RFC 4655`, `RFC-4655`, `BCP 38`, `STD 5`, `I-D`, and a linked `RFC 4655 § 4` with its no-break spaces), which keeps out an IANA registration's `URI: urn:…` and an explanation after the expansion; what is left gets at most two more content words than the short form has letters, because a definition can be a sentence that happens to hold the letters. Bibliography entries and verbatim blocks are not read. The RPC's abbreviation list, the fallback for what a document never expands, waits on the licensing question in `DATA_PIPELINE.md`.
- **Defined terms are collected at parse time as well** (`DefinedTerms`, issue #176), into `RFCDocument.definedTerms`, keyed by the term as written, the first definition winning. Two sources, strictest first: a primary index entry (`<iref primary="true">`) without a subitem, defined by the block it sits in at that block's anchor or its section's (one in a `<dt>` by the `<dd>` after it) (a subitem files a name under an index group, `Grammar` / `ALPHA`, which is not a term); and the definition lists of a section titled for its terms and of its subsections, since most definition lists describe fields or notation. A title names terms when it holds Terminology, Glossary or the noun Terms (`New Terms`, `Terms Used in This Document`), is `Conventions and` followed by Definitions, Terminology, Terms or Acronyms, opens with Definitions, is `Definition of Terms`, or lists Definitions as an item of its own (`Symbols, Abbreviations, and Definitions`). `Conventions and Notation` describes notation, as a bare `Conventions` does. Definitions qualified by a word before it is a format's, an option's or a media type's as often as a document's terms, and `General` or `Technical` cannot be told apart from `Field` or `Option`, so all of them are left out. Measured over the corpus, that and dropping the singular took 484 terms out of 41 documents, nearly all of them fields, parameters and registration-template entries; the genuine terminology left out with them sits under a qualified title. A list nested in a definition describes that term's parts and is not read (21 terms in 7 documents); one set in a list item is. An index entry that has no definition text, one placed directly in a section, gives way to an entry that has, so a term an index marks and a terminology list defines lands on the list item. A subsection inherits its parent's title, because a terminology section splits its lists into `Core Terms`, `Abbreviations` or `Roles` as readily as it titles them for terms; that brought in 903 terms in 55 documents, most of them abbreviation lists, at the cost of 25 that are not terms: RFC 8891 and 9374's notation symbols, and the graph notation and per-term Definition/Discussion headings of RFC 9912, 9914 and 9971. A title that only opens with Definitions passes nothing on, since it can head a specification's body: it brought in RFC 8985's connection variables and timers and RFC 9208's command arguments (23 terms), and nothing else. The noun Terms brought in 424 terms in 30 documents, all but nine of them terminology (RFC 8985's two variables and RFC 9224's imported JSON grammar names). Narrowing `Conventions and` took nothing out: the four documents titled `Conventions and Notation`, `…Assumptions` or `…Licenses` hold no definition list there. Free prose is not read. Over the corpus, 466 documents define 5,991 terms. How precise that is has not been measured, and has to be before a reader marks terms in prose: a term can carry what its `<dt>` carries, a citation or the start of its definition.
- **An author's contact details are data, not body text** (#19, #113). `Author.contact` holds what RFCXML's `<author>` publishes: `<organization>` and an `<address>` with its `<postal>`, phone, fax, emails and URI. `PostalAddress` keeps whichever of the schema's two forms the author chose: a field per element, a building (`<extaddr>`) apart from a street and a district (`<cityarea>`) apart from a city, or the author's own `<postalLine>`s, and the serializer writes the same form back. The URI is kept as written, so one `URL` cannot read is shown as text rather than lost. The `ascii` transliterations beside these are not kept: the reader shows the document's own script, and the schema makes them optional. `contact` is nil for every author a legacy header or the RFC index names, since nothing is looked up or inferred. Prep's "Authors' Addresses" section is built from the same elements, and a Contributors section from `<contact>`, which has the same content. Those were read as unknown containers, an aside for each level of `<author><address><postal>`, which the schema rejects on a round trip, and as inline names run together, the rest dropped. Both now read as one paragraph per person, a detail per line, with email and web addresses as links, and an organization that is its own entry's name is not repeated beneath it. The parser's fallback for an unknown container, which sets several blocks apart as an aside of its own, splices an aside inside that one rather than nesting it. The header shows them as a contact card: each author is a chip, and one that publishes anything beyond its name opens Apple's `CNContactViewController` on an unsaved contact that `AuthorCard` (RFCReaderKit) builds from exactly these fields, the author's own postal lines kept as written and a sorting code after the city, where France writes a CEDEX, since Contacts has no field for it (#19). The chip's monogram is white initials on a tint picked by an FNV-1a hash of the name, and text drawn on a color the code picks is held to a calculated contrast ratio: WCAG 2.x relative luminance and contrast, computed by `SRGBColor` (RFCReaderKit), against the 4.5:1 Apple's Human Interface Guidelines ask of text at standard sizes, since initials are small bold text and the 3:1 for large text does not apply. The system colors cannot meet it with white — yellow measures 1.5:1, mint 2.1:1, only indigo passes — and resolve differently by appearance and platform, so `AuthorMonogram.palette` is fixed sRGB: each system hue darkened in linear light until white clears 4.5:1, which keeps the hue and, the circle being opaque, the same contrast in light and dark appearance. Switching the initials to black on the light tints was the alternative, and passes too, but would draw seven of the eight chips differently from the eighth. The inspector's Info pane (#25) lists the authors with the same chips.
- **A mention is linked in whatever spelling the document used.** `RFC 1156`, `RFC-1156` and `RFCs 734, 736 and 749` all name documents, and the series has written all three since the 1980s. The hyphen and the list were worth the patterns: measured over the corpus's prose, 1,640 mentions were of the plain shape, 2,223 hyphenated and 659 in lists. What the reference *reads* as is still not the parser's to decide — `isCanonicalTag` says `RFC 1156` is the series spelling its own name and composes back, while the hyphen in `RFC-1156` and the bare number in a list are the author's and survive verbatim.
- **Both parsers run `InlineLinker` over prose.** *Decided September 2026.* Marking up a citation is the author's choice, not the reader's: authored XML says "RFC 3986" in the middle of a sentence as readily as it writes `<xref>`, 160 times in RFC 9293 alone, and until the XML parser ran the same linker the whole post-8650 range showed those as plain text while the legacy range linked them. One linker, for the same reason there is one `isCanonicalTag`: two of them disagree, and the same reference then reads differently depending on which format it arrived in. It is suppressed exactly where the text is already a link (`<eref>`'s content) or is set as typed (`<tt>`, `<sourcecode>`, `<artwork>`, and every preformatted block, which never becomes inlines at all).
- **A heading is inlines, not a string.** *Decided September 2026.* Headings cite documents — "Changes from RFC 3066", "Differences from RFC 793", some 3,500 of them across the corpus — and a `String` title could not carry the link however well the linker worked. `Section.title` is `[Inline]`; `titleText` and `displayTitle` flatten it for the outline, and `displayTitleInlines` is what the reader draws. Anchors are built from the section number, not from the words. The number is never part of the words: it lives in `number` and the reader composes `4.2. ` around whatever the heading says, so the linker cannot mistake a heading's own number for a section reference.
- **Display text travels with the reference.** RFCXML's prepped output carries `derivedContent` ("Figure 1", "Section 4.2"); the parser reuses it rather than re-implementing numbering rules. The same goes for the bibliography: a `<reference>` carries the tag its citations print as `derivedAnchor` — `[HTTP]` for RFC 9110 under `<displayreference>`, `[1]` under `symRefs="false"`, half the modern series between them — and `Reference.displayAnchor` is that tag, while `anchor` stays the link key. The one thing it restyles is a bare canonical number (`RFC9110` → `RFC` + U+00A0 + `9110`); an author's own tag (`QUIC-TRANSPORT`) is left exactly as the document writes it.

## Parsing the two source formats

**RFCXML v3** (`RFCXMLParser`): the whole file is loaded into a tiny DOM (`XMLTree`) and walked. Documents are at most a couple of megabytes; RFC 9110 (1.2 MB, 305 sections, 1,376 paragraphs) parses in about 50 ms in a release build. Whitespace inside `<t>` is collapsed as HTML would; artwork and source code are kept byte-for-byte. A `<u>` element is spelled out as its `format` asks (by default `"ש" (HEBREW LETTER SHIN, U+05E9)`), with the literal as code, because the vocabulary requires the code point in every rendering and the bare character depends on a font (`UnicodeNotation`, issue #63). The vocabulary also lets a `<u>` carry an `anchor`, so that an `<xref>` to it shows the character's name and code point at the reference while the `<u>` itself shows only the literal. That split is deliberately not supported: the anchor is ignored, and the `<u>` is spelled out in place like any other. On 2026-10-03, 11 of the 9,835 documents in the converted corpus used `<u>` and none gave one an anchor (#213); revisit when an RFC does. `<artset>` picks the ASCII alternative until there is an SVG renderer.

**Legacy text** (`LegacyTextParser`), in order:
1. *Depaginate*: drop form feeds, `[Page N]` footers and the running header that follows a page break; remember where breaks were. Then drop the lines that recur at the page edges however they are worded (RFC 793's `Transmission Control Protocol`) — but only a line set off from the body by a blank and seen at the edges more often than inside the pages, because the body recurs too and a MIB's `STATUS current` lands at a page edge by chance. A line on half the pages names the document — half the pages of its parity, where headers alternate between facing pages (RFC 821). A section running header must head every page, or every other, of three or more, as part of the block that opens the page: the body starts below the blank lines after a page's header, and RFC 6208's `Additional information:` at the head of five pages is the body's. A line wrongly kept costs a stray heading; one wrongly dropped is lost text, so the rule errs towards keeping. Depagination does not decide a section running header's first sighting, which may be the only thing saying where its section starts (RFC 770's `References`) or a copy of a heading the document sets itself (RFC 793's `Philosophy`): it tags it (`Line.sectionHeader`), and `prepared` drops it later, through `Prelude`, where the document heads that section with the same words on the header's page or the two before it (#291). `corpus/report.json` records each document's dropped lines as `furniture`, because the block counts cannot show that loss.
2. *Front matter*: the two-column header block (`Request for Comments: 1149`, `Obsoletes:`, `Category:`, authors, date) up to the first blank line, then the centered title. It runs on to the first run that is the body's — one holding a column-0 heading, or a paragraph before that — and ends at that run's start; with no heading at all, it ends after the title. Front matter has nowhere to put a paragraph, so running on to the first heading lost everything above it (#60: RFC 809's 1,951 lines up to its appendix).
3. *Headings*: any non-indented line. `1.2.` → numbered; `Appendix B.` / `B.1.` → appendix; anything else unnumbered. Depth from the number; sections nest with a stack. `1:` and `2.4.12:` are numbered too, where the document numbers its headings that way (#71: RFC 2078, 2743 and 2130 all through, five others for a few). The same shape is a field label, a line-numbered listing or a second numbering elsewhere, so it counts only where two things hold across the document: no colon number is also the number of a `1.` heading, since a document numbers each section once (RFC 705 lists `1.  BEGIN Command` and describes it again under `1:  BEGIN`); and every colon number follows from a heading number — the one before it at its level, or one it is under — because a lone `11:` with no `10` is a time (RFC 526), not a numbering. A line ending in a contents leader and a page number (`2.  Overview ....... 5`) opens no section, wherever it sits (the front matter's end still counts it as a heading, so no front matter moves): RFC 791, 793 and the specifications set like them list their contents at column 0, and every entry opened a section (#403). Those documents center their chapter headings instead (`1.  INTRODUCTION`) and set only the subsections at column 0; a numbered line off column 0 is a heading where it stands alone, no heading has taken its number, and the next heading is its first subsection. Anywhere else it is a list item.
4. *Blocks*: runs of lines separated by blank lines. A block is prose if every line shares a small indent and contains no artwork tell-tales (`+--`, `|`, `->`, runs of spaces); prose is reflowed. A block starting with `o`, `-`, `*`, `1.`, `(a)` becomes a list. Everything else is preformatted, minus common indent. Blocks cut by a page break are re-joined when both halves look like prose and the first half does not end a sentence, or the second opens lower case; a trailing hyphen joins without a space. A second half that opens with a bullet is never joined, however the first ends (RFC 1581, 6614) — except a dash, which can continue a sentence (RFC 2123's `- e.g. its SourcePeerAddress - is ...`), and is refused only where the block above opens with a bullet too. A block indented past a list's marker and carrying no marker of its own is that item's next paragraph: the indent cap is excused, because the list above says what the indent means, but every other test of prose still applies — and one of them has to be `readsLikeSentences`, or the algorithm steps and tagged values that fill those indents are swallowed into the item as prose.
5. *References*: `[Anchor] text…` entries with hanging indents; RFC/BCP/STD numbers, quoted title, date and URL are pulled from the text. An anchor may hold spaces (`[RFC 2119]`, `[Cheswick and Bellovin, 1994]`) and is capped at 40 characters, which is what separates a citation tag from a bracketed sentence.
6. *Boilerplate* ("Status of This Memo", "Table of Contents", copyright) is dropped from the model but stays in the "original text" view, which is `stripPagination(_:)` over the same file. A dropped section, or the abstract, ends at the next heading, so where the body's headings are a shape the parser does not know it would take the body with it (#60, RFC 908: 60 pages inside `Status of This Memo`). One that runs past 20 blocks — no boilerplate in the corpus is more than 16 — keeps only its leading paragraphs, or contents entries, and the rest is body.

**Serializing back** (`RFCXMLSerializer`): the document model can be written out as RFCXML v3 using the RFC Editor's conventions (`pn` part numbers, anchors). This is how the corpus pipeline turns legacy text into XML once, offline, so the app needs only the XML path at runtime. Cross references to documents without a bibliography entry become `<eref>`s to rfc-editor.org, which the parser resolves back into document references; the round trip is tested on both a text-derived and a native XML document.

The index (`RFCIndexParser`) is different: 14 MB and flat, so it is a streaming SAX state machine rather than a DOM. About one second for its 9,842 RFCs (`DATA_PIPELINE.md` has the counts, dated).

Both XML parsers ignore a parser error reported *after* the root element has closed. swift-corelibs-foundation emits one on large inputs even for valid XML (verified with `xmllint`); a truncated file still fails because its root never closes, and there is a test for that.

## Data flow in the app

```
RFC Editor ──HTTP──▶ RFCEditorClient ──────────bytes──▶ DocumentStore (actor)
                                                          │  writes rfcNNNN.xml/.txt to Application Support
                                                          │  parses on demand, memoizes RFCDocument
                                                          ▼
                       LibraryModel (@Observable, one per process) ── index, search, lists, document cache, open tabs
                                                          ▼
                       NavigationModel (one per tab) ── filter, search text, selection, back/forward history, scroll request
                       ReaderState (one per window)  ── what the reader shows, for the toolbar and the inspector
                                                          ▼
                       SwiftUI views ── macOS: NSSplitViewController(sidebar, list, DocumentView, panel)
                                        iOS:   NavigationSplitView(sidebar, list, DocumentView)
                                                          ▼
                       SwiftData ── Bookmark, ReadingPosition, DocumentCollection, DocumentCollectionItem (RFCReaderKit's UserData; user data only; iCloud later)
```

- Legacy RFCs arrive pre-converted to XML through data packs (DATA_PIPELINE.md); on-device text parsing is the fallback when no pack is installed.
- A document is fetched by `RFCEditorClient.fetchPreferredDocument`, the one fallback rule (#125): the XML where the index lists it, and the text only when there is no XML, a 404 for it. A canceled load, a server error or a network failure is the error, not a reason to start a second request whose failure would be reported instead. XML that is there but does not parse falls back to the text too, and the parse error comes back beside the document for `DocumentStore` to log; with no text either, the parse error is what is thrown.
- Raw files are cached exactly as served. Re-parsing after a parser fix is free, and the "original text" mode needs no second download.
- The index is cached to disk and checked in the background when it was last checked over a day ago: a conditional request with the kept `ETag`, so an unchanged index is a `304` that only records the check (`IndexCheck`). The `ETag` is sent for a week after the index was downloaded and then dropped, so a tag that failed to change cannot hide a new index for longer. Nobody is waiting for the check, so it waits for a network that is neither expensive nor constrained (#314); a bundled snapshot (drop `rfc-index.xml` into the app's resources) makes first launch work offline.
- Navigation is data, and a tab's own. `NavigationModel.open(_:in:)` moves the tab's history to a `HistoryEntry` — a document and, optionally, a section — and sets `selection` and a `scrollRequest`; `DocumentView` scrolls once the document has loaded. A link from inside a tab goes through `LibraryModel.open(_:activation:in:)`, which opens it in that tab or in a new one ([the per-tab navigation decision](decisions/2026-09-22-per-tab-navigation-and-how-a-tab-gets-opened.md)). A link from outside — the URL scheme or the App Intent — has no tab, and `LibraryModel.route(_:)` picks one: the tab already showing the document, else on macOS the tab of the window that was key last (#277), else the one used last, else a new window (`LinkRouting`, in `RFCReaderKit`). Which tabs are open, most recently used first, and the one link waiting for a tab are `SceneRegistry`'s (#137): a link waits until there is a tab to take it, and a BCP or STD link until the index has arrived, or failed to, as well, so on a cold launch it opens its first RFC rather than selecting the series (#241). Only series links wait for the index because only they need it to resolve; making every link wait was set aside because no index is bundled with the app, and a first launch would hold every link for the index's whole download. The library never reaches for the window layer: on macOS it asks `WindowOpening`, which `AppDelegate` answers.
- Cross references are `.link` attributes in the built text, with three private schemes: `rfc://…` for other documents, `rfc-anchor:…` for a place in the same one, and one for a bibliography entry, which the body leaves out and the inspector shows. The text view's delegate hands a clicked link to `DocumentView` with the click's `LinkActivation` — `clickedOnLink` on macOS, `primaryActionFor` on iOS — and `LinkDestination.resolve` decides where it goes; what it does not understand falls through to the system. `DocumentView` installs no `OpenURLAction` of its own: every other link under it is a page on the web, and one that did read rfc-editor.org's pages as the RFCs they name (#450).

## Testing

`swift test --package-path Packages/RFCKit` runs RFCKit's Swift Testing suites in a few seconds on the committed fixtures: RFC documents in both formats, a trimmed index, the RSS feed and a per-RFC JSON record. Each parser has a truncated-input test. `swift test --package-path Packages/RFCReaderKit` (`make test-app`) runs the app-side package's own suites — `DocumentTextBuilder`, the chip and completeness guards, `FragmentGeometry`, `ReaderLayout`, platform helpers — against the same harness; it needs an Apple SDK, so `make check` runs it only on a Mac.

The App target has no test bundle of its own, so **nothing that can be tested is allowed to live there**. The rule that keeps this honest: anything in the reader that is a pure function of its inputs belongs in `RFCReaderKit`, and the App target keeps only what genuinely needs UIKit/AppKit object graphs — the two representables, the coordinator's view wiring, and drawing. Where a decoration goes (`FragmentGeometry`), how wide the column is (`ReaderLayout`) and what the text says (`DocumentTextBuilder`) are all in the package, under test. This is not cosmetic: both of the reader's hardest bugs were index arithmetic that had been written in the App target, where the only thing a test could do was re-implement it and check the copy.

CI runs `Packages/RFCKit`'s tests on both macOS (`.github/workflows/ci-macos.yml`) and in a Linux Swift container (`.github/workflows/ci.yml`); the macOS job also runs `make test-app` for `RFCReaderKit`. A third job builds the app itself, unsigned, for macOS and the iOS Simulator with Xcode 26.6, and a fourth does the same with Xcode 27 on GitHub's preview image, reported without failing the run. The macOS jobs are a workflow of their own so that a draft pull request skips them; marking it ready for review runs them, without re-running the Linux checks of the same commit. The corpus-backed suites run in their own workflow (`corpus-tests.yml`), on every pull request that touches `Packages/RFCKit`, `Tools/corpus-build` or the Makefile, and weekly.

## Planned engines

- **Search.** As [the search decision](decisions/2026-09-24-full-text-search-ranks-by-measurement-and-was-measured-before-it-was-built.md) says, served from SQLite FTS5 (via GRDB), with snippets from `snippet()`. Metadata search moves into the same database.
- **Highlighting.** Built for JSON, XML and HTTP messages on one regex lexer engine in RFCKit (`Lexer`, `Lexers`), [as decided](decisions/2026-10-02-syntax-highlighting-is-one-regex-lexer-engine.md); further languages are rule tables on it. ABNF's rule links come from the strict `ABNF` parser instead (#185), and untyped blocks are not guessed at.
- **Diff.** Section alignment from `SectionAlignment` (title and prose, ties broken by position; the `successions` table for an obsoletes edge), LCS over paragraphs within aligned sections, word-level diff (`CollectionDifference` or Myers) inside changed paragraphs. Output is a diff document rendered with the same block views plus insert/delete styling. Works for draft revisions and for obsoleted RFC → successor.
- **Diagrams.** Box-art to Unicode box-drawing conversion per block; packet-diagram parser producing a bit-field model rendered natively.

## The TextKit 2 traps this reader already fell into

*Found September and October 2026, all by measuring — a running build, or a probe over the real builder's output — rather than by reading.*

**`NSTextContentStorage.attributedString = …` discards the backing `NSTextStorage`.** `NSTextContentStorage.install(_:)` in `RFCReaderKit` is the way in, and `StorageInstallTests` pins both it and the trap. Assigning it is the obvious way to install a document and it renders perfectly, because TextKit 2 lays out and draws from `attributedString` alone. But `textStorage` goes nil, and with it everything AppKit still routes through the text storage: dragging computed a correct selection and threw it away at mouse-up, and `clickedOnLink` never fired. The reader could be read but not selected, copied or clicked, with no error anywhere. `install(_:)` writes through `textStorage?.setAttributedString(_:)` inside an editing transaction.

**`attribute(_:at:effectiveRange:)` returns the storage run, not the attribute's run.** It stops at *any* attribute change, so a stacked table's bold label and regular value are separate runs even though both carry the same `.rfcDecoration`. Every fragment then reported itself as both the first and last fragment of its decoration, drawing a fully rounded card at its own indent — the staircase down the page that artwork, tables and authors' blocks all showed. `longestEffectiveRange:in:` is the one that coalesces. Artwork hid this for months because its attributes were uniform; since its last line alone carries the block's spacing (#31), it depends on the coalescing as much as a table does. Two corollaries: the value has to be `String`-backed to compare equal across insertions (`RFCDecoration.attributeValue`), and every character of a block has to carry it — an undecorated separator newline splits the run just as effectively as a missing attribute.

**A text container that tracks the text view's width throws the whole layout away on every resize.** The column is centered with the inset, and the frame and the inset cannot change in one step, so a tracking container passes through a width that is neither the old column nor the new one — and TextKit discards every fragment frame for it and lays the viewport out again from estimates at the unmoved scroll offset. Measured on RFC 9000, a resize that only moved the gutters left the reader 39,000 characters further on, with no rebuild coming to put it back. The coordinator sizes the container to the column in `layOut(width:measure:)`, so only a change of column re-wraps.

**A TextKit 2 text view's subviews outlive it on macOS 27, and hold the whole document (#356).** The text view goes when the reader moves to another document, but the content view that draws its text and the viewport element views of the fragments on screen stay, registered with the notification center by blocks from `-[NSView _commonAwake]` that capture them. Through the content view they kept the layout manager, the storage and every layout fragment: after ten opens, one text view but eleven storages and 27,803 fragments, 35 MB a document never freed, and from the eighth open every build 5 to 10 times slower. A plain `NSTextView` in a small program leaks the same way, so it is AppKit's. What the subviews reach is ours to cut: `dismantleNSView` detaches the text view from its container (`RFCTextViewCoordinator.releaseDocument()`, and the same line in `OriginalTextBody`), which releases the layout manager and everything below it. Measured after it: two storages whatever the number of opens and build times flat. The element views are still AppKit's to keep, and add up: up to a dozen more with every open, one fragment each, a few kilobytes where the document was 35 MB. Emptying the storage instead left the layout manager and storage behind, and removing the subviews left the fragments.

**`relocateViewport(to:)` does not hold a line, and a point lookup after a relocation answers a stale fragment.** In the layout engine's probe, `NSTextViewportLayoutController.relocateViewport(to:)` after a prior relocation put its target at y = 0 or collapsed the viewport range: 1 of 40 jumps landed exactly, and its suggested anchor point was off by 300 to 25,000 characters. `textLayoutFragment(for: CGPoint)` returned a fragment from before the relocation. The engine uses neither: it pins by scrolling (`PinRecipe`) and reads what is at the top from the viewport's own fragments, enumerated from `viewportRange`.

**TextKit drops the layout of the whole document on its own.** Scrolling back up RFC 5661 on an iPhone, at the same place every run, one fragment came back 100 pt taller and every fragment in the document went back to estimated heights: the height fell from 836,956 to 739,555 pt. No invalidation was asked for, nothing about the container had changed, and TextKit's own `NSTextLayoutFragment` did the same. Its positions are never reliably exact for long, even after the whole document was laid out; the engine relies only on a drop not moving what is above the reader.

**A jump that does not lay out what is above its target leaves two sets of coordinates.** The viewport stays where TextKit estimated it, and the layout from the document's start later puts the same line elsewhere — about 48,000 pt apart in the middle of RFC 5661 — and neither gives way: the reader ended up 73,000 characters from the line, whatever background layout did when it reached the viewport. `PinRecipe.settle` is the rule: lay out from the start through the target's paragraph, then pin.

**UIKit's viewport walks from where it last put its text.** A scroll on iOS moves the viewport from the fragment it last laid at its top, by the distance scrolled from the y it laid it at; it neither asks the layout manager what is at the new y nor notices that the fragment has moved since. A settle's layout from the start moves that fragment wherever what was above it had been estimated, so the scroll to the target walked from a stale y: on an iPhone, the pin, the caret and the reader's place all put RFC 9000's §15 at the top while the screen showed text 9,000 characters on, and 80,000 on in the issue's worst case (#625). The pin could not see it: the target's own fragment is where the pin put it, the viewport is elsewhere. A jump from a viewport at the top of the document always landed, and that is the fix: `PinRecipe.settle` first scrolls to the document's start, where the first fragment is at 0 in both, and walks from there. The Mac landed every jump without it.

**A forced viewport layout during a live resize makes `NSTextView` lay out a large range itself.** Pinning on every step of a drag is cheap, but calling `layoutViewport()` after the pin went through `textViewportLayoutControllerDidLayout:` into `ensureLayoutForRange:` (sampled): 500–630 ms per step on RFC 5661, against under 1 ms without. The engine leaves the viewport to AppKit's own display pass during a live resize.

## Known gaps and the next technical steps

- Legacy text: a definition list set as xml2rfc sets one, `Term:  definition` with the rest hung under it, is read as one (#436), but a term on a line of its own with its definition under it (e.g. the cache directives in RFC 2616 §14.9.1) still renders as a preformatted block; nested lists are flattened; multi-author front matter picks up only authors that sit on their own line.
- Search covers the index's metadata only, in memory, scanning every entry per query (the "Search" benchmarks of `make benchmark`). Full-text search over document bodies is [the search decision](decisions/2026-09-24-full-text-search-ranks-by-measurement-and-was-measured-before-it-was-built.md), not yet built (#37); metadata search moves into its database when it lands.
- SVG artwork (`<artwork type="svg">`) is skipped in favor of the ASCII alternative.
- No Spotlight indexing or iCloud sync yet. Errata and the Datatracker are pages the Info pane links to, not data the app fetches.
- The reader lays out its viewport and completes the rest in the background; a deep jump before completion has passed its target still lays out everything above it first (#295). See [Decision: the reader lays out its viewport, and holds the reader's line](decisions/2026-09-30-the-reader-lays-out-its-viewport-and-holds-the-readers-line.md).
- On macOS, VoiceOver says a diagram as one label, "Diagram", in place of its box drawing. Not `Preformatted.name`, which is RFCXML's file name to extract the artwork to, not a title. A diagram is artwork that is a drawing (`AccessibleReading.isDiagram`): at least 0.3 of its characters other than white space draw lines, boxes or arrows, and no more than half of its non-blank lines are words — a letter, and more than half letters and digits — because legacy documents set every block that is not prose as artwork, and a grammar, a message example or a table reads perfectly well as words. A bordered table therefore needs more rows than borders to be read; one with a header and two data rows is still said as a diagram, because a bit layout alternates field rows and borders just as closely. The model telling them apart is #361. `ReaderTextView` overrides the per-range accessibility accessors, which hand their `super` to `AccessibleReading.reading` (RFCReaderKit), while `accessibilityValue` stays the real text, because every range VoiceOver asks for is measured in its length. The label is said where a range reaches the diagram's first character, so read a line at a time the first line announces it and the rest are silent. It is not the figure's caption, which in a document from XML is set as text right after the diagram and would be read twice; a legacy document keeps its "Figure 3: …" line inside the artwork, where it goes unsaid with the drawing until #361 splits it out into a real title. Reading through, VoiceOver no longer reaches the ASCII; only what bypasses the per-range accessors still carries it, `accessibilityValue` and `accessibilitySelectedText`. The Diagrams rotor jumps to a diagram and lists it by its figure's caption (`.rfcCaption`, `AccessibleReading.rotorLabel`), which the rotor never reads through, or as "Diagram" without one; Copy Figure puts the ASCII on the pasteboard. On iOS, `UITextView` has no per-range accessor to override, so the builder says it in the text instead (#308): a diagram's first line that is not blank carries the IPA of "diagram" as `accessibilitySpeechIPANotation`, and its later lines an empty pronunciation (`AccessibleReading.diagramSpeech`, in reader builds only), line breaks and blank lines nothing. Only "diagram", since a packet diagram's fields cannot be made IPA. Whether VoiceOver honors a speech attribute in a text view's storage, reading through and line by line, is not documented, and is to be confirmed by ear on a device, as is what character and word navigation inside a diagram's line says now: the pronunciation covers the whole line, so it may be said for every character of it. Why it is said in the text and not by an accessibility element of its own is [the iOS diagram speech decision](decisions/2026-10-02-on-ios-a-diagram-is-said-by-a-pronunciation-in-the-text.md).
- A block folded per RFC 8792 is shown unfolded, without its header, when every unfolded line fits the column at full size, and otherwise exactly as the page printed it, header and backslashes included (`DocumentTextBuilder.displayedText`, issue #64). A block that does not fit is not scaled down to fit unfolded, because its unfolded lines can be far longer than the widths fit-to-measure scaling was measured against. "Copy Figure" always copies it unfolded (`FoldedLines` in RFCKit, `FigureCopy` in RFCReaderKit). A plain-text copy of a selection over a block shown folded is unfolded too, joining each fold whose backslash and continuation are both selected and leaving out a selected header (`FoldedLines.unfold(selection:strategy:)` from `SelectionText`, #212). The rich flavors keep the folded lines.
