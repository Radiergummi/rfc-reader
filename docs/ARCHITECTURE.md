# Architecture

## Shape of the code base

```
└── docs/
```

The split is the point: **RFCKit knows nothing about SwiftUI, and the app knows nothing about XML or the 72-column text format.** The boundary is the `RFCDocument` model. That keeps the hard, testable logic on a fast Linux/macOS test loop and lets the UI be rewritten (TextKit 2, a different navigation model, visionOS) without touching parsing.

## The document model

```
RFCDocument
├── header: DocumentHeader          title, id, authors, date, abstract blocks, keywords, obsoletes/updates, precedingDraft
├── sections: [Section]             tree; each has anchor, number ("4.2", "A.1"), title: [Inline], blocks, subsections
└── source: .xml | .text

Block (enum)
  paragraph(Paragraph)              inlines + optional anchor + author's indent in characters
  list(ListBlock)                   bullet / numbered(ListNumbering) / bare; items hold blocks
  definitionList(DefinitionList)    items (term inlines + definition blocks); compact, and whether terms hang beside their definitions
  preformatted(Preformatted)        artwork or sourceCode, verbatim text, optional language
  figure(Figure) · table(Table) · blockQuote · aside
  references(ReferenceList)         bibliographic entries with resolved DocumentID where possible, and annotation inlines; its kind (normative, informative, unknown) is derived from its title

Inline (enum)
  text · emphasis · strong · code · superscript · subscript · link(URL) · crossReference · lineBreak

CrossReference.target
  .anchor(String)                                          same document
  .document(DocumentID, section: String?, entry: String?)  another RFC, optionally a section, and the bibliography entry the citation resolved to
  .entrySection(entry:, tag:, section:, url: URL?)          a section of a bibliography entry outside the series, and that section's page
```

Design choices worth knowing:

- **Anchors are stable strings**, not indices: the author's `anchor` or the RFC Editor's `pn` in XML, and in legacy text `section-4.2`, `appendix-A` or `name-…`, with paragraphs numbered in prep's shape (#491). Deep links, the table of contents and reading positions key off them, and whatever a document can cite keeps one, down to a `<dd>` and a table row (#166); `Block.anchors` lists a block's own. How each kind is made, and how a bibliography entry's label becomes one: [the reference decision](decisions/2026-09-24-the-parsers-do-not-decide-how-a-reference-reads.md).
- **Cross references are resolved at parse time.** Both parsers index the bibliography first, every list of it wherever it sits, then link the prose: `<xref target="QUIC-TRANSPORT">` becomes `.document(RFC9000)`, and the text parser links `[Anchor]`, `RFC 1234`, `Section 4.2 of [RFC9110]`, `Section 3` (where that section exists) and URLs. The serializer writes a legacy document so that the XML parser reads the same model back, and its output is a fixed point (`SerializerRoundTripTests`): [the decision](decisions/2026-10-17-the-serializer-writes-what-the-xml-parser-reads-back.md).
- **Abbreviations are collected at parse time too** (`Abbreviations`, #67), into `RFCDocument.abbreviations`, keyed by the short form: from `Transport Layer Security (TLS)` at first use and from a glossary's `TLS: Transport Layer Security`, the first expansion winning. The long form is found with Schwartz and Hearst's algorithm, tightened by rules measured over the corpus, and a document that never expands an abbreviation gets none: [the decision](decisions/2026-10-17-an-abbreviation-is-expanded-only-where-the-document-expands-it.md).
- **Defined terms are collected at parse time as well** (`DefinedTerms`, #176), into `RFCDocument.definedTerms`, keyed by each spelling prose would use (`DefinedTerms.spellings(of:)`, #396), from primary index entries and from the definition lists of sections titled for their terms. Which titles count, and what was measured: [the decision](decisions/2026-10-14-a-defined-term-is-a-gloss-at-its-first-use-in-a-paragraph.md).
- **A document's index is a block of its own** (`Block.index(IndexBlock)`): letter groups of entries, each a term with its locators and its subentries, a locator a `CrossReference` and whether it is the primary one. Only prep generates one, from an RFCXML document's `<iref>`s, so `RFCXMLParser` reads a section as an index only where its first block is the `<t>` prep anchors `rfc.index.index`, and only in prep's shape; anything else reads as the generic blocks it is made of. Prep writes an item's own locators two ways when it has subitems, in its `<dd>` (RFC 9110) or in a subentry without a term (RFC 9051, 9499), and both give them to the item. A term is a name, so an RFC number in it is not linked as a citation (RFC 9051 indexes `RFC822.SIZE`). The serializer writes the block back in prep's shape, a fixed point. Its terms are prose to the traversals, its locators are not, so a mention in the index is not one of a section's backlinks. The reader sets it as the plain blocks it read as before (`DocumentTextBuilder.plainBlocks(of:)`), with its letters now leading to their groups, until it is set as an index (the design in `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-05-document-index-design.md`).
- **An author's contact details are data, not body text** (#19, #113). `Author.contact` holds what `<author>` publishes, organization and address in whichever form the author chose (`PostalAddress`), and is nil for an author a legacy header or the index names. Prep's Authors' Addresses and Contributors sections read as one paragraph per person. The header's author chips open a contact card (`AuthorCard`), and their monograms are held to 4.5:1 (`AuthorMonogram.palette`, `SRGBColor`): [the decision](decisions/2026-10-17-an-authors-contact-details-are-data-shown-as-a-contact-card.md).
- **A mention is linked in whatever spelling the document used.** `RFC 1156`, `RFC-1156` and `RFCs 734, 736 and 749` all name documents, and the series has written all three since the 1980s. The hyphen and the list were worth the patterns: measured over the corpus's prose, 1,640 mentions were of the plain shape, 2,223 hyphenated and 659 in lists. What the reference *reads* as is still not the parser's to decide — `isCanonicalTag` says `RFC 1156` is the series spelling its own name and composes back, while the hyphen in `RFC-1156` and the bare number in a list are the author's and survive verbatim.
- **Both parsers run `InlineLinker` over prose.** *Decided September 2026.* Marking up a citation is the author's choice, not the reader's: authored XML says "RFC 3986" in the middle of a sentence as readily as it writes `<xref>`, 160 times in RFC 9293 alone, and until the XML parser ran the same linker the whole post-8650 range showed those as plain text while the legacy range linked them. One linker, for the same reason there is one `isCanonicalTag`: two of them disagree, and the same reference then reads differently depending on which format it arrived in. It is suppressed exactly where the text is already a link (`<eref>`'s content) or is set as typed (`<tt>`, `<sourcecode>`, `<artwork>`, and every preformatted block, which never becomes inlines at all).
- **A heading is inlines, not a string.** *Decided September 2026.* Headings cite documents — "Changes from RFC 3066", "Differences from RFC 793", some 3,500 of them across the corpus — and a `String` title could not carry the link however well the linker worked. `Section.title` is `[Inline]`; `titleText` and `displayTitle` flatten it for the outline, and `displayTitleInlines` is what the reader draws. Anchors are built from the section number, not from the words. The number is never part of the words: it lives in `number` and the reader composes `4.2. ` around whatever the heading says, so the linker cannot mistake a heading's own number for a section reference.
- **Display text travels with the reference.** RFCXML's prepped output carries `derivedContent` ("Figure 1", "Section 4.2"); the parser reuses it rather than re-implementing numbering rules. The same goes for the bibliography: a `<reference>` carries the tag its citations print as `derivedAnchor` — `[HTTP]` for RFC 9110 under `<displayreference>`, `[1]` under `symRefs="false"`, half the modern series between them — and `Reference.displayAnchor` is that tag, while `anchor` stays the link key. The one thing it restyles is a bare canonical number (`RFC9110` → `RFC` + U+00A0 + `9110`); an author's own tag (`QUIC-TRANSPORT`) is left exactly as the document writes it.

## Parsing the two source formats

**RFCXML v3** (`RFCXMLParser`): the whole file is loaded into a tiny DOM (`XMLTree`) and walked. Documents are at most a couple of megabytes; RFC 9110 (1.2 MB, 305 sections, 1,376 paragraphs) parses in about 50 ms in a release build. Whitespace inside `<t>` is collapsed as HTML would; artwork and source code are kept byte-for-byte. A `<u>` element is spelled out as its `format` asks (by default `"ש" (HEBREW LETTER SHIN, U+05E9)`), with the literal as code, because the vocabulary requires the code point in every rendering and the bare character depends on a font (`UnicodeNotation`, issue #63). The vocabulary also lets a `<u>` carry an `anchor`, so that an `<xref>` to it shows the character's name and code point at the reference while the `<u>` itself shows only the literal. That split is deliberately not supported: the anchor is ignored, and the `<u>` is spelled out in place like any other. On 2026-10-03, 11 of the 9,835 documents in the converted corpus used `<u>` and none gave one an anchor (#213); revisit when an RFC does. `<artset>` picks the ASCII alternative until there is an SVG renderer. An artwork published only as SVG, an `<artset>` without an ASCII alternative or a bare `<artwork type="svg">`, keeps its type and shows what xml2rfc's text rendering sets in its place, "(Artwork only available as SVG: see" the RFC Editor's page), rather than its drawing's text nodes run together (#768).

**Legacy text** (`LegacyTextParser`), in order:
1. *Depaginate*: drop form feeds, `[Page N]` footers and the running headers and footers that recur at the page edges, remembering where the breaks were, and erring towards keeping a line. A section running header's first sighting is tagged (`Line.sectionHeader`) and dropped later only where the document heads that section with the same words (#291). `corpus/report.json` records the dropped lines as `furniture`. [The details](decisions/2026-10-17-how-the-legacy-parser-recovers-structure.md).
2. *Front matter*: the two-column header block (`Request for Comments: 1149`, `Obsoletes:`, `Category:`, authors, date) up to the first blank line, then the centered title. It runs on to the first run that is the body's — one holding a column-0 heading, or a paragraph before that — and ends at that run's start; with no heading at all, it ends after the title. Front matter has nowhere to put a paragraph, so running on to the first heading lost everything above it (#60: RFC 809's 1,951 lines up to its appendix).
3. *Headings*: any non-indented line. `1.2.` → numbered; `Appendix B.` / `B.1.` → appendix; anything else unnumbered; depth from the number, sections nested with a stack. `1:` and `1)` are numbered only where the rest of the document's numbering bears them out (#71, #199); a contents entry opens no section (#403); and in documents that center their chapter headings, a centered numbered or capitals line can be a chapter (#550). [The details](decisions/2026-10-17-how-the-legacy-parser-recovers-structure.md).
4. *Blocks*: runs of lines separated by blank lines. A block is prose if every line shares a small indent and has no artwork tell-tales, and prose is reflowed; one starting with `o`, `-`, `*`, `1.`, `(a)` is a list; everything else is preformatted, minus common indent. Blocks cut by a page break are joined again where both halves read as prose, the cut falls mid-sentence, and the second half does not open with a bullet. A drawing is typed `ascii-art` (`DrawingShape`, #361), and a `Figure 3: Title` caption under a verbatim block makes it a `Figure` (`LegacyTextParser+Figures`). [The details](decisions/2026-10-17-how-the-legacy-parser-recovers-structure.md).
5. *References*: a section whose title names references as a word, outside parentheses, is a bibliography when the title is only that (`References`, `Normative References:`) or when its entries outnumber the blocks before the first of them; any other is prose that mentions references, like RFC 7322's advice on writing a references section (#686). A bibliography holds bibliographies and nothing else, because `<references>` has room for nothing else: a section the numbering nests under one (RFC 2814's `A.1` after `9. References`, its `Appendix A` heading missing) follows it instead, with every subsection after it. Entries are `[Anchor] text…` with hanging indents; RFC/BCP/STD numbers, quoted title, date and URL are pulled from the text. An anchor may hold spaces (`[RFC 2119]`, `[Cheswick and Bellovin, 1994]`) and is capped at 40 characters, which is what separates a citation tag from a bracketed sentence.
6. *Boilerplate* ("Status of This Memo", "Table of Contents", copyright) is dropped from the model but stays in the "original text" view, which is `stripPagination(_:)` over the same file. A dropped section, or the abstract, ends at the next heading, so where the body's headings are a shape the parser does not know it would take the body with it (#60, RFC 908: 60 pages inside `Status of This Memo`). One that runs past 20 blocks — no boilerplate in the corpus is more than 16 — keeps only its leading paragraphs, or contents entries, and the rest is body.

**Serializing back** (`RFCXMLSerializer`): the document model can be written out as RFCXML v3 using the RFC Editor's conventions (`pn` part numbers, anchors). This is how the corpus pipeline turns legacy text into XML once, offline, so that with a pack installed the app needs only the XML path at runtime. Cross references to documents without a bibliography entry become `<eref>`s to rfc-editor.org, which the parser resolves back into document references; the round trip is tested on both a text-derived and a native XML document.

The index (`RFCIndexParser`) is different: 14 MB and flat, so it is a streaming SAX state machine rather than a DOM. About one second for its 9,842 RFCs (`DATA_PIPELINE.md` has the counts, dated).

Both XML parsers ignore a parser error reported *after* the root element has closed. swift-corelibs-foundation emits one on large inputs even for valid XML (verified with `xmllint`); a truncated file still fails because its root never closes, and there is a test for that.

## Data flow in the app

```
RFC Editor ──HTTP──▶ RFCEditorClient ──────────bytes──▶ DocumentStore (actor)
                                                          │  writes rfcNNNN.xml/.txt to Application Support
                                                          │  parses on demand, memoizes RFCDocument
                                                          ▼
                       LibraryModel (@Observable, one per process) ── index, search, lists, document cache, open tabs
                                                          ▼
                       NavigationModel (one per tab) ── filter, search text, selection, back/forward history, scroll request
                       ReaderState (one per window)  ── what the reader shows, for the toolbar and the inspector
                                                          ▼
                       SwiftUI views ── macOS: NSSplitViewController(sidebar, list, DocumentView, [beside,] panel)
                                        iOS:   NavigationSplitView(sidebar, list, ReaderStack of DocumentViews [| beside])
                                                          ▼
                       SwiftData ── Bookmark, ReadingPosition, DocumentCollection, DocumentCollectionItem (RFCReaderKit's UserData; user data only; iCloud later)
```

- Legacy RFCs arrive pre-converted to XML through data packs (DATA_PIPELINE.md); on-device text parsing is the fallback when no pack is installed.
- A document is fetched by `RFCEditorClient.fetchPreferredDocument`, the one fallback rule (#125): the XML where the index lists it, and the text only when there is no XML, a 404 for it. A canceled load, a server error or a network failure is the error, not a reason to start a second request whose failure would be reported instead. XML that is there but does not parse falls back to the text too, and the parse error comes back beside the document for `DocumentStore` to log; with no text either, the parse error is what is thrown.
- Raw files are cached exactly as served. Re-parsing after a parser fix is free, and the "original text" mode needs no second download.
- The index is cached to disk and checked in the background when it was last checked over a day ago: a conditional request with the kept `ETag`, so an unchanged index is a `304` that only records the check (`IndexCheck`). The `ETag` is sent for a week after the index was downloaded and then dropped, so a tag that failed to change cannot hide a new index for longer. Nobody is waiting for the check, so it waits for a network that is neither expensive nor constrained (#314); a Release build bundles the index (`make bundled-index` fetches it into the gitignored `App/Resources`, and project.yml's "Bundle the RFC index" phase copies it, failing a Release build without it), so a first launch lists every RFC offline and the check replaces it, dated when it was fetched. A Debug build bundles it only when it is there; without one, a first launch shows the download, or why it failed, in place of the list (`IndexPlaceholder`, #770).
- Every change to the index or to `revisions.json` compares the bookmarked RFCs with the last comparison (#191): `BookmarkBaseline` records what each says of them, `BookmarkEvents` reports what changed since (obsoleted, updated, a draft revising one or reaching the RFC Editor queue, errata now listed), and `BookmarkNotice` words one local notification per RFC, which opens its `rfc://` link. The baseline is kept per device in Application Support and never synced, so a new device, or a newly bookmarked RFC, reports nothing from before; a comparison without an index or revisions keeps the last it had of them. The comparison runs whether notifications are on or not, so turning them on starts from then. The switch is in the Mac's Settings; iOS gets it with its Settings screen (#703), and until then its half is built but never turned on. Besides the refreshes the app makes anyway, iOS asks for a daily `BGAppRefreshTask` while notifications are on, and macOS refreshes daily for as long as the app runs, with no login item.
- A document can be read beside the one it obsoletes, or the one obsoleting it (#187). The reader beside is a second `DocumentView` with its own `NavigationModel` and `ReaderState`, held by the window's reader state as a `SideBySide`: a fifth split item on macOS, the detail column split in two on iPad, not on iPhone. The sections are aligned on the device with `SectionAlignment`, and the two readers scroll together through `ScrollCoupling` and `AlignedScrolling` in RFCReaderKit: the reader used last leads, and the other follows to the counterpart of its section ([the decision](decisions/2026-10-04-a-document-read-beside-another-is-a-fifth-split-item.md)).
- Navigation is data, and a tab's own. `NavigationModel.open(_:in:)` moves the tab's history to a `HistoryEntry` — a document and, optionally, a section — and sets `selection` and a `scrollRequest`; `DocumentView` scrolls once the document has loaded. Whether an open jumps within the document on screen or moves the history, and whether it reveals the document in the list, is `OpenRoute`; the scroll request, and the script waiting on a jump it asked for, are `ScrollRequests` (both in `RFCReaderKit`, #773). A link from inside a tab goes through `LibraryModel.open(_:activation:in:)`, which opens it in that tab or in a new one ([the per-tab navigation decision](decisions/2026-09-22-per-tab-navigation-and-how-a-tab-gets-opened.md)). A link from outside — the URL scheme or the App Intent — has no tab, and `LibraryModel.route(_:)` picks one: the tab already showing the document, else on macOS the tab of the window that was key last (#277), else the one used last, else a new window (`LinkRouting`, in `RFCReaderKit`). Which tabs are open, most recently used first, and the one link waiting for a tab are `SceneRegistry`'s (#137): a link waits until there is a tab to take it, and a BCP or STD link until the index has arrived, or failed to, as well, so on a cold launch it opens its first RFC rather than selecting the series (#241). The library never reaches for the window layer: on macOS it asks `WindowOpening`, which `AppDelegate` answers.
- App Intents (#192) act on three entities: `RFCEntity`, `SectionEntity` (identified across documents as `rfc9110#section-4.2`, `SectionIdentifier`) and `RegistryEntryEntity`. Their queries are RFCKit's `DocumentLookup`, `SectionLookup`, `Requirements.within` and `RegistryLookup`, tested there; the App target holds only the conformances, which ask `LibraryModel` for the index once it has settled (`settledSearch()`), a document as the reader loads it, and the registries. Each Spotlight item carries its RFC's entity (`SpotlightIndexer`), so a Spotlight result is the RFC Shortcuts and Siri act on. An intent that shows something beside a document, as Find Requirements' button shows the Requirements tab, routes its link and leaves a `DocumentRequest` (RFCReaderKit), which the reader of that document takes once its panel can open. What the intents say back is worded in RFCReaderKit's `IntentAnswer`.
- A `DocumentView` and its text view talk through one object, `ReaderController` (RFCReaderKit, #598), which the view owns and hands to the representable. The coordinator reports into it in two halves: at once, outside observation, what cannot wait — the place and the section's anchor, which the reading position saves on the way out, the header's heading and the toolbar's title, which is coupled to the scroll; and after one hop, observed, the section, the selection, the bars on iPhone and a folding the text view changed itself. The hop is there because the coordinator reports from inside SwiftUI's update, installing a build or jumping, where changing observed state is illegal; it is made in one place, `publish`, rather than at every report. `DocumentView` writes the observed half into the window's `ReaderState` through its `Writer`, so `ReaderState.currentAnchor` is the window's copy of `controller.section`. Requests go the other way: `scroll(to:offset:)` reaches the text view at once when it has a build installed, through `ReaderTextDriving`, and otherwise waits for the install to carry it out, once. A stateless action — a link followed, a presentation chosen, a preview committed — stays a closure on `ReaderInputs`. On iPhone the bars are `ReaderBars`, the UIKit half of `ReaderChrome`, as `ReferenceHoverController` is of `ReferenceHover`.
- On iOS the detail column is a stack of readers (`ReaderStack`, #263): a citation of another RFC followed in a reader pushes one, and the system back button returns to the one below, kept where it was left. The stack is not a second history but a projection of the tab's: each `HistoryEntry` says how the tab arrived at it, from outside the reader (a row, Go to RFC, a link from another app) or by a link followed inside it, and `ReaderPath` stacks a reader for each run of entries in one document since the last arrival from outside. The stack's own back is handed to the history (`NavigationHistory.popReaders(to:leaving:)`). The window's `ReaderState` is the top reader's: a reader below writes nothing to it from its load, its builds or its text view's reports, which go through a `ReaderState.Writer` that writes only while `NavigationModel.shows(_:at:)` it (`ScopedWriter`, #772). It puts its document's details back when it is on top again (`DocumentSession.reinstate`). The eight readers nearest the top keep their builds; deeper ones are made again if popped back to. The list selects the stack's root (`NavigationModel.listSelection`), and the panel is an inspector on the split view, for the reasons the decision gives. The Mac has one reader.
- Cross references are `.link` attributes in the built text, with three private schemes: `rfc://…` for other documents, `rfc-anchor:…` for a place in the same one, and one for a bibliography entry, which the body leaves out and the inspector shows. The text view's delegate hands a clicked link to `DocumentView` with the click's `LinkActivation` — `clickedOnLink` on macOS, `primaryActionFor` on iOS — and `LinkDestination.resolve` decides where it goes; what it does not understand falls through to the system. `DocumentView` installs no `OpenURLAction` of its own: every other link under it is a page on the web, and one that did read rfc-editor.org's pages as the RFCs they name (#450).

## Testing

`swift test --package-path Packages/RFCKit` runs RFCKit's Swift Testing suites in a few seconds on the committed fixtures: RFC documents in both formats, a trimmed index, the RSS feed and a per-RFC JSON record. Each parser has a truncated-input test. `swift test --package-path Packages/RFCReaderKit` (`make test-app`) runs the app-side package's own suites — `DocumentTextBuilder`, the chip and completeness guards, `FragmentGeometry`, `ReaderLayout`, platform helpers — against the same harness; it needs an Apple SDK, so `make check` runs it only on a Mac.

The App target has no test bundle of its own, so **nothing that can be tested is allowed to live there**. The rule that keeps this honest: anything in the reader that is a pure function of its inputs belongs in `RFCReaderKit`, and the App target keeps only what genuinely needs UIKit/AppKit object graphs — the two representables, the coordinator's view wiring, and drawing. Where a decoration goes (`FragmentGeometry`), how wide the column is (`ReaderLayout`) and what the text says (`DocumentTextBuilder`) are all in the package, under test. This is not cosmetic: both of the reader's hardest bugs were index arithmetic that had been written in the App target, where the only thing a test could do was re-implement it and check the copy.

CI runs `Packages/RFCKit`'s tests on both macOS (`.github/workflows/ci-macos.yml`) and in a Linux Swift container (`.github/workflows/ci.yml`); the macOS job also runs `make test-app` for `RFCReaderKit`. A third job builds the app itself, unsigned, for macOS and the iOS Simulator with Xcode 26.6, runs RFCReaderKit's tests on the iOS Simulator (`make test-app-ios`), and checks that the string catalogs hold what those builds found, with every key translated into German (`make strings-check`). A fourth job makes the same two builds with Xcode 27 on GitHub's preview image, without the catalog check, and is reported without failing the run. The macOS jobs are a workflow of their own so that a draft pull request skips them; marking it ready for review runs them, without re-running the Linux checks of the same commit. The corpus-backed suites run in their own workflow (`corpus-tests.yml`), on every pull request that touches `Packages/RFCKit`, `Tools/corpus-build` or the Makefile, and weekly.

## Planned engines

- **Search.** As [the search decision](decisions/2026-10-07-full-text-search-is-built-on-the-device-and-ranked-flat.md) says, SQLite FTS5 built on the device, through RFCKit's `CSQLite`, one row per section with snippets from `snippet()` (`FullTextIndex`, #37). `TextIndexer` (RFCReaderKit) keeps it to the bodies the store holds, and the search shows its section hits "In the Text" under the RFC rows. Index All RFCs (`TextIndexer.indexAll`, run by `IndexAllRun`) indexes every RFC without keeping its body, reading the legacy pack's XML where one is installed, and marks each *retained*, which is the one exception to the index following the store: a retained document stays when its body is evicted, until Remove Full-Text Index releases them. Metadata search moves into the same database.
- **Highlighting.** Built for JSON, XML and HTTP messages on one regex lexer engine in RFCKit (`Lexer`, `Lexers`), [as decided](decisions/2026-10-02-syntax-highlighting-is-one-regex-lexer-engine.md); further languages are rule tables on it. ABNF's rule links come from the strict `ABNF` parser instead (#185), and untyped blocks are not guessed at.
- **Diff.** Section alignment from `SectionAlignment` (title and prose, ties broken by position; the `successions` table for an obsoletes edge), LCS over paragraphs within aligned sections, word-level diff (`CollectionDifference` or Myers) inside changed paragraphs. Output is a diff document rendered with the same block views plus insert/delete styling. Works for draft revisions and for obsoleted RFC → successor.
- **Diagrams.** Box-art to Unicode box-drawing conversion per block; packet-diagram parser producing a bit-field model rendered natively.

## The TextKit 2 traps this reader already fell into

*Found September and October 2026, all by measuring — a running build, or a probe over the real builder's output — rather than by reading.*

**`NSTextContentStorage.attributedString = …` discards the backing `NSTextStorage`.** `NSTextContentStorage.install(_:)` in `RFCReaderKit` is the way in, and `StorageInstallTests` pins both it and the trap. Assigning it is the obvious way to install a document and it renders perfectly, because TextKit 2 lays out and draws from `attributedString` alone. But `textStorage` goes nil, and with it everything AppKit still routes through the text storage: dragging computed a correct selection and threw it away at mouse-up, and `clickedOnLink` never fired. The reader could be read but not selected, copied or clicked, with no error anywhere. `install(_:)` writes through `textStorage?.setAttributedString(_:)` inside an editing transaction.

**`attribute(_:at:effectiveRange:)` returns the storage run, not the attribute's run.** It stops at *any* attribute change, so a stacked table's bold label and regular value are separate runs even though both carry the same `.rfcDecoration`. Every fragment then reported itself as both the first and last fragment of its decoration, drawing a fully rounded card at its own indent — the staircase down the page that artwork, tables and authors' blocks all showed. `longestEffectiveRange:in:` is the one that coalesces. Artwork hid this for months because its attributes were uniform; since its last line alone carries the block's spacing (#31), it depends on the coalescing as much as a table does. Two corollaries: the value has to be `String`-backed to compare equal across insertions (`RFCDecoration.attributeValue`), and every character of a block has to carry it — an undecorated separator newline splits the run just as effectively as a missing attribute.

**A text container that tracks the text view's width throws the whole layout away on every resize.** The column is centered with the inset, and the frame and the inset cannot change in one step, so a tracking container passes through a width that is neither the old column nor the new one — and TextKit discards every fragment frame for it and lays the viewport out again from estimates at the unmoved scroll offset. Measured on RFC 9000, a resize that only moved the gutters left the reader 39,000 characters further on, with no rebuild coming to put it back. The coordinator sizes the container to the column in `layOut(width:measure:)`, so only a change of column re-wraps.

**A TextKit 2 text view's subviews outlive it on macOS 27, and hold the whole document (#356).** The text view goes when the reader moves to another document, but the content view that draws its text and the viewport element views of the fragments on screen stay, registered with the notification center by blocks from `-[NSView _commonAwake]` that capture them. Through the content view they kept the layout manager, the storage and every layout fragment: after ten opens, one text view but eleven storages and 27,803 fragments, 35 MB a document never freed, and from the eighth open every build 5 to 10 times slower. A plain `NSTextView` in a small program leaks the same way, so it is AppKit's. What the subviews reach is ours to cut: `dismantleNSView` detaches the text view from its container (`RFCTextViewCoordinator.releaseDocument()`, and the same line in `OriginalTextBody`), which releases the layout manager and everything below it. Measured after it: two storages whatever the number of opens and build times flat. The element views are still AppKit's to keep, and add up: up to a dozen more with every open, one fragment each, a few kilobytes where the document was 35 MB. Emptying the storage instead left the layout manager and storage behind, and removing the subviews left the fragments.

**`relocateViewport(to:)` does not hold a line, and a point lookup after a relocation answers a stale fragment.** In the layout engine's probe, `NSTextViewportLayoutController.relocateViewport(to:)` after a prior relocation put its target at y = 0 or collapsed the viewport range: 1 of 40 jumps landed exactly, and its suggested anchor point was off by 300 to 25,000 characters. `textLayoutFragment(for: CGPoint)` returned a fragment from before the relocation. The engine uses neither: it pins by scrolling (`PinRecipe`) and reads what is at the top from the viewport's own fragments, enumerated from `viewportRange`.

**TextKit drops the layout of the whole document on its own.** Scrolling back up RFC 5661 on an iPhone, at the same place every run, one fragment came back 100 pt taller and every fragment in the document went back to estimated heights: the height fell from 836,956 to 739,555 pt. No invalidation was asked for, nothing about the container had changed, and TextKit's own `NSTextLayoutFragment` did the same. Its positions are never reliably exact for long, even after the whole document was laid out; the engine relies only on a drop not moving what is above the reader.

**A jump that does not lay out what is above its target leaves two sets of coordinates.** The viewport stays where TextKit estimated it, and the layout from the document's start later puts the same line elsewhere — about 48,000 pt apart in the middle of RFC 5661 — and neither gives way: the reader ended up 73,000 characters from the line, whatever background layout did when it reached the viewport. `PinRecipe.settle` is the rule: lay out from the start through the target's paragraph, then pin.

**UIKit's viewport walks from where it last put its text.** A scroll on iOS moves the viewport from the fragment it last laid at its top, by the distance scrolled from the y it laid it at; it neither asks the layout manager what is at the new y nor notices that the fragment has moved since. A settle's layout from the start moves that fragment wherever what was above it had been estimated, so the scroll to the target walked from a stale y: on an iPhone, the pin, the caret and the reader's place all put RFC 9000's §15 at the top while the screen showed text 9,000 characters on, and 80,000 on in the issue's worst case (#625). The pin could not see it: the target's own fragment is where the pin put it, the viewport is elsewhere. A jump from a viewport at the top of the document always landed, and that is the fix: `PinRecipe.settle` first scrolls to the document's start, where the first fragment is at 0 in both, and walks from there. The Mac landed every jump without it.

**A forced viewport layout during a live resize makes `NSTextView` lay out a large range itself.** Pinning on every step of a drag is cheap, but calling `layoutViewport()` after the pin went through `textViewportLayoutControllerDidLayout:` into `ensureLayoutForRange:` (sampled): 500–630 ms per step on RFC 5661, against under 1 ms without. The engine leaves the viewport to AppKit's own display pass during a live resize.

## Known gaps and the next technical steps

- Legacy text: a definition list whose term stands on a line of its own still renders as a preformatted block, nested lists are flattened, and an author line set near the left margin of the header is not read.
- Search covers the index's metadata in memory, and finds sections through the full-text index (`FullTextIndex`, kept by `TextIndexer`) beside it; moving the metadata search into the full-text database is #37.
- SVG artwork is not drawn, by design until there is an SVG renderer: an `<artset>` shows its ASCII alternative, and an artwork with none names the RFC Editor's page that draws it (decided in #768).
- Spotlight indexes RFCs, not their sections (#178). There is no iCloud sync. The Datatracker is a page the Info pane links to, and errata are listed in the Info pane and marked in the reader by a chip after the heading of each section they name, or by the title for those naming none (`ErrataMarks`, an input to the build, #387).
- By design, a deep jump made before the background layout has passed its target lays out everything above it first (decided in #295, [the decision](decisions/2026-09-30-the-reader-lays-out-its-viewport-and-holds-the-readers-line.md)).
- How VoiceOver says a diagram, as one label on macOS and as a pronunciation in the text on iOS, is to be confirmed by ear on a device (#942; [the decision](decisions/2026-10-02-on-ios-a-diagram-is-said-by-a-pronunciation-in-the-text.md)).
- By design, a block folded per RFC 8792 whose unfolded lines do not fit the column is shown folded, as printed, rather than scaled down (decided in #64, [the decision](decisions/2026-10-17-a-folded-block-is-shown-unfolded-where-it-fits.md)).
